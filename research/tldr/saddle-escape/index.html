A Theory of Saddle Escape in Deep Nonlinear Networks

When do neural networks first learn features? [arXiv]

(Part of a series of short writeups covering recent work.)

Generally, when a network is trained from small initialization (i.e. all its weights are initially very small), we tend to see a pretty characteristic pattern: long plateaus where loss barely drops, with sharp drops as new features are acquired. This "saddle-to-saddle" structure is pretty well understood for deep linear networks (where the activation function is Id⁡:x↦x\operatorname{Id}: x \mapsto xId:x↦x). For nonlinear functions, why this happens remains mysterious; here we try to provide a result that shows that deep nonlinear networks are not that different than their linear counterparts.

Background#

Deep Linear Networks#

Much (I'd even say most) of the existing theory for optimization dynamics is based in the deep linear network model: an LLL-layer network without any activation function, just a long chain of matrix multiplications1 y^=WL⋯W1x\hat y = W_L \cdots W_1 xy^​=WL​⋯W1​x. Although the model is simple, deep linear networks exhibit surprisingly nontrivial dynamics during gradient flow (W˙=−∇WL\dot W = - \nabla_W \mathcal LW˙=−∇W​L) and many features of deep learning (e.g. saddle-to-saddle structure, low-rank bias, progressive feature learning) appear here in an analytically tractable form [SMG14].

A lot of the reason deep linear networks are so nice to analyze is due to the conservation law of the imbalance of the weight matrix norms between adjacent layers: Δℓ≐∥Wℓ+1∥F2−∥Wℓ∥F2. \Delta_\ell \doteq \|W_{\ell+1}\|_F^2 - \|W_\ell\|_F^2. Δℓ​≐∥Wℓ+1​∥F2​−∥Wℓ​∥F2​. For linear networks, one can show Δ˙ℓ=0\dot \Delta_\ell = 0Δ˙ℓ​=0 for all ℓ\ellℓ [DHL18]. Intuitively, the network only ever sees the composition Wℓ+1 σ(Wℓh)W_{\ell+1}\,\sigma(W_\ell h)Wℓ+1​σ(Wℓ​h), so if σ\sigmaσ is linear we can shrink WℓW_\ellWℓ​ by any factor ccc and grow Wℓ+1W_{\ell+1}Wℓ+1​ by 1/c1/c1/c without changing the output function. The loss then has a flat direction, and so gradient flow cannot move along a direction in which the loss doesn't change, so whatever that rescaling moves is frozen.

In a broader sense, this follows for any activation function that satisfies Euler's homogeneity identity: z⋅σ′(z)=σ(z)z \cdot \sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)z⋅σ′(z)=σ(z) for σ(z)=z\sigma(z) = zσ(z)=z.2 So, if we define the functional φσ(z)≐zσ′(z)−σ(z), \varphi_\sigma(z) \doteq z\sigma'(z) - \sigma(z), φσ​(z)≐zσ′(z)−σ(z), for a homogeneous function, it vanishes exactly. This is actually a much bigger deal than it seems; this conserved quantity is what makes linear network analysis work, and later we will use the physicist's favorite trick of Taylor expanding about the origin to show a nonlinear analog of this!

Nonlinear Activations#

For a general nonlinear activation σ\sigmaσ, φσ≢0\varphi_\sigma \not\equiv 0φσ​≡0 and our neat conservation law is not guaranteed.

To remedy this, let us study the Taylor expansion of φσ\varphi_\sigmaφσ​ near zero. Writing σ(u)=αu+aquq+O(uq+1)\sigma(u) = \alpha u + a_q u^q + O(u^{q+1})σ(u)=αu+aq​uq+O(uq+1) with α=σ′(0)>0\alpha = \sigma'(0) > 0α=σ′(0)>0, the linear part cancels in φσ\varphi_\sigmaφσ​ (that is what Euler's identity says), and the first surviving term has order qqq: φσ(z)=(q−1) aqzq+O(zq+1). \varphi_\sigma(z) = (q-1)\,a_q z^q + O(z^{q+1}). φσ​(z)=(q−1)aq​zq+O(zq+1). For tanh⁡\operatorname{tanh}tanh, σ(z)≈z−z3/3\sigma(z) \approx z - z^3/3σ(z)≈z−z3/3, so φtanh⁡(z)=z sech2(z)−tanh⁡(z)≈−23z3\varphi_{\tanh}(z) = z\,\mathrm{sech}^2(z) - \tanh(z) \approx -\tfrac{2}{3}z^3φtanh​(z)=zsech2(z)−tanh(z)≈−32​z3, giving q=3q = 3q=3. That's kinda the main thing. Near the origin (where our network is initialized since we take initialization tending to zero), the pre-activations (i.e. the values passed into the activation) are small, so φσ(z)≈cqzq\varphi_\sigma(z) \approx c_q z^qφσ​(z)≈cq​zq is high order small. Thus, the conservation law is only "slightly broken" and we can track exactly its drift to order qqq, which gives us enough control to do what we want to with our analysis. A similar argument in principle is the physics small angle approximation; sin⁡θ≠θ\sin\theta \neq \thetasinθ=θ, but the error is cubic, so for small swings we may use the linear theory and carry a correction to be bounded later.

Imbalance and Drift#

Consider an LLL-layer network y^=WLσ(WL−1⋯σ(W1x)⋯ )\hat y = W_L \sigma(W_{L-1} \cdots \sigma(W_1 x) \cdots)y^​=WL​σ(WL−1​⋯σ(W1​x)⋯) with pre-activations zℓ≐Wℓσ(zℓ−1)z_\ell \doteq W_\ell \sigma(z_{\ell-1})zℓ​≐Wℓ​σ(zℓ−1​) and population loss L\mathcal{L}L.

Theorem 1. For any smooth activation σ\sigmaσ and any differentiable loss L\mathcal{L}L, dΔℓdt=2 E ⁣[⟨Wℓ+1⊤∇zℓ+1L,  φσ(zℓ)⟩]. \frac{d\Delta_\ell}{dt} = 2\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\bigl\langle W_{\ell+1}^\top \nabla_{z_{\ell+1}} \mathcal{L},\; \varphi_\sigma(z_\ell) \bigr\rangle\right]. dtdΔℓ​​=2E[⟨Wℓ+1⊤​∇zℓ+1​​L,φσ​(zℓ​)⟩].

The proof is quite natural; it follows by considering scaling before and after activation is applied; we outline it below.

Scaling layer ℓ\ellℓ and scaling layer ℓ+1\ell+1ℓ+1 both act on the same object zℓ+1=Wℓ+1σ(zℓ)z_{\ell+1} = W_{\ell+1}\sigma(z_\ell)zℓ+1​=Wℓ+1​σ(zℓ​), but they come in different places: one multiplies the argument of σ\sigmaσ, the other multiplies its output. Differentiating each at s=0s=0s=0, dds[Wℓ+1 σ((1+s)zℓ)]=Wℓ+1(zℓ⊙σ′(zℓ)),dds[(1+s)Wℓ+1 σ(zℓ)]=Wℓ+1 σ(zℓ). \frac{d}{ds}\Big[W_{\ell+1}\,\sigma\bigl((1+s)z_\ell\bigr)\Big] = W_{\ell+1}\bigl(z_\ell \odot \sigma'(z_\ell)\bigr), \qquad \frac{d}{ds}\Big[(1+s)W_{\ell+1}\,\sigma(z_\ell)\Big] = W_{\ell+1}\,\sigma(z_\ell). dsd​[Wℓ+1​σ((1+s)zℓ​)]=Wℓ+1​(zℓ​⊙σ′(zℓ​)),dsd​[(1+s)Wℓ+1​σ(zℓ​)]=Wℓ+1​σ(zℓ​). Some important comments on the above: (1) the identity is exact and assumes basically nothing, and (2) the right-hand side is a correlation between the upstream gradient at layer ℓ+1\ell+1ℓ+1 and φσ\varphi_\sigmaφσ​ applied to the pre-activations at layer ℓ\ellℓ. When φσ≡0\varphi_\sigma \equiv 0φσ​≡0 we recover exact conservation; when it doesn't vanish, the drift is controlled by how large φσ(zℓ)\varphi_\sigma(z_\ell)φσ​(zℓ​) is, and near the saddle that is O(εq)O(\varepsilon^q)O(εq).3

Note that we are using an approximation that is valid near the origin in order to analyze the event whose entire purpose is to take the network away from the origin. The resolution is that "escape" is declared at a small fixed threshold rather than at the end of training, and the time integral we take is dominated by its earliest part, where the network is deepest inside the regime the approximation covers.4 Most of the actual work in the paper is showing that the defect stays negligible along the entire trajectory, and not just at initialization.

Reducing to One Number#

Approximate conservation buys us what exact conservation bought the linear theory, with a controlled error. If the bottleneck layers start at comparable scales, they stay comparable for the whole escape, so instead of tracking rrr matrices we can track one scalar: their shared gain.

This is the conservation law doing its job. Those layer gains have no particular reason to agree with each other, and once the escape is over they visibly don't, but through the transition itself they move as one.

The other half of the reduction is that near the saddle every layer's gradient collapses to rank one, and every layer points along the same rank-one direction. The network is not being pushed in many directions at once; it is being pushed in one. So the right scalar to track alongside the gain is the network's overlap with that direction, call it γ\gammaγ, and the two of them chase each other: Y˙≍Γ γ,γ˙≳Γ Y r−1, \dot Y \asymp \Gamma\,\gamma, \qquad \dot\gamma \gtrsim \Gamma\, Y^{\,r-1}, Y˙≍Γγ,γ˙​≳ΓYr−1, where YYY is the first bottleneck gain and Γ\GammaΓ is the strength of the signal the data presents (we will pin it down in a moment). This is a feedback loop, and it reads like one: the network can only grow its channel as fast as it is correlated with the target, and it can only become correlated with the target as fast as the channel lets signal through.5 Near the origin both are tiny, which is the entire reason the plateau exists. Integrating the loop is what gives the escape time.

The Critical-Depth Law#

Suppose rrr of the LLL layers initialize at scale ε→0+\varepsilon \to 0^+ε→0+ (call these the bottleneck layers) and the remaining L−rL - rL−r initialize at scale Θ(1)\Theta(1)Θ(1). By the reduction above, all rrr bottleneck layers share a magnitude y(t)y(t)y(t).

Here is the point. The gradient driving any one bottleneck layer is the product of the signals passing through all of the other bottleneck layers, so it scales as yr−1y^{r-1}yr−1, not yry^ryr: a layer does not appear in its own gradient. The L−rL-rL−r full-size layers contribute O(1)O(1)O(1) factors and only move the prefactor. So the scalar ODE for the shared bottleneck magnitude is y˙≍Γ y r−1, \dot y \asymp \Gamma\, y^{\,r-1}, y˙​≍Γyr−1, and the time to get from y(0)=εy(0) = \varepsilony(0)=ε out to a small fixed threshold ϑ\varthetaϑ is one integral: τ≍1Γ∫εϑy−(r−1) dy. \tau \asymp \frac{1}{\Gamma}\int_\varepsilon^\vartheta y^{-(r-1)}\,dy. τ≍Γ1​∫εϑ​y−(r−1)dy. Everything follows from whether that integral is finite at 000, log divergent, or power divergent.

Theorem 2 (Critical-Depth Escape Law). As ε→0+\varepsilon \to 0^+ε→0+, the escape time satisfies τ={Θ(Γ−1)r=1Θ(Γ−1log⁡(1/ε))r=2Θ(Γ−1ε−(r−2))r≥3. \tau = \begin{cases} \Theta\bigl(\Gamma^{-1}\bigr) & r = 1 \\[2pt] \Theta\bigl(\Gamma^{-1}\log(1/\varepsilon)\bigr) & r = 2 \\[2pt] \Theta\bigl(\Gamma^{-1}\varepsilon^{-(r-2)}\bigr) & r \geq 3. \end{cases} τ=⎩⎨⎧​Θ(Γ−1)Θ(Γ−1log(1/ε))Θ(Γ−1ε−(r−2))​r=1r=2r≥3.​

The −(r−2)-(r-2)−(r−2) exponent has a pretty neat interpretation: one power of ε\varepsilonε is consumed because each layer's gradient is yr−1y^{r-1}yr−1 rather than yry^ryr, and a second is absorbed by the integral. The threshold at r=2r=2r=2 is special, since it is the minimal bottleneck for which the escape time diverges at all as ε→0\varepsilon \to 0ε→0; one small layer is asymptotically free, and two cost you only a logarithm, which is the familiar statement that growth proportional to current size is just compound interest.

It is worth putting numbers on this. Take ε=0.01\varepsilon = 0.01ε=0.01 and ϑ=0.1\vartheta = 0.1ϑ=0.1. The integral evaluates to about 0.090.090.09, 2.32.32.3, 909090, and 495049504950 for r=1,2,3,4r = 1, 2, 3, 4r=1,2,3,4. Going from two small layers to four, at the same initialization scale and with everything else held fixed, costs you a factor of more than two thousand in wall-clock time.

Now look at what is absent from that theorem. The width does not appear. The total depth LLL does not appear. The particular smooth activation does not appear. The number of distinct features in the target does not appear. None of these are obviously irrelevant: width multiplies the number of directions the flow could move in, extra depth multiplies the number of compositions, and swapping the activation reshapes the loss landscape. They are absent anyway, and the reason is the rank-one structure from the previous section. The escape from the saddle is not a high-dimensional event. It is a one-dimensional one wearing a high-dimensional costume, and the only things that survive the reduction are an integer and a scalar.

What the Data Contributes#

That leaves Γ\GammaΓ, and there is a clean argument for what it has to be. Near the origin the activations are all evaluated close to zero, where σ(u)≈αu\sigma(u) \approx \alpha uσ(u)≈αu, so the network is a linear map to leading order. A linear map can only correlate with the linear part of the target. Everything else about the target, every nonlinearity in it, is invisible to the network until it has already moved. So the only property of the data that can possibly set the initial clock is the plain input-output correlation: Γ=∥E[yx]∥. \Gamma = \bigl\|\mathbb{E}[yx]\bigr\|. Γ=​E[yx]​. One vector moment. Not the input dimension, not how many features the target has, not how nonlinear they are.

The part of this that is genuinely counterintuitive is that Γ\GammaΓ is a vector sum. If the target is built from several features vkv_kvk​ with amplitudes βk\beta_kβk​, each one contributes a vector ∝βkuka^k\propto \beta_k u_k \hat a_k∝βk​uk​a^k​, where a^k\hat a_ka^k​ is the direction of Σvk\Sigma v_kΣvk​, and these add with signs: Γ=hσ∥∑kβkuka^k∥. \Gamma = h_\sigma\Bigl\|\textstyle\sum_k \beta_k u_k \hat a_k\Bigr\|. Γ=hσ​​∑k​βk​uk​a^k​​. Two features whose covariance images point in opposing directions partially cancel one another. Rotate them toward cancellation and Γ\GammaΓ falls, and the network sits on the plateau proportionally longer, even though neither feature has become any weaker on its own. Any summary that scored the features independently and added up their strengths would predict no change at all. This is also the one place where the input covariance really earns its keep: a teacher direction that is misaligned with Σ\SigmaΣ gets presented to the network more strongly than its own variance would suggest.6

The practical consequence is nicer than the theory strictly requires. Because Γ\GammaΓ is just a correlation, you never need to decompose the target into features to compute it, and nothing in the definition cares whether the expectation is over a population or a finite sample. On a real dataset it is Γ^=n−1∑iyixi\hat\Gamma = n^{-1}\sum_i y_i x_iΓ^=n−1∑i​yi​xi​, which is one line of numpy, and in the paper that empirical quantity predicts escape times on fixed MNIST and CIFAR-10 covariates across datasets, targets, and initialization scales, with a single prefactor calibrated once.

Do Features Arrive One at a Time?#

The staircase in the opening figure invites an obvious reading: one step, one feature. Our theory says when the network leaves the first saddle, but the picture above makes it tempting to assume that departure is really several departures, one per feature, that we have averaged together. It isn't.

With a scalar output, the network near the origin is driven along a single direction, so it cannot grow several independent channels for the simple reason that it is not being pushed along several independent directions. Every feature overlap grows through one common amplitude, and the ratios between them are fixed in advance by the target and the input covariance, independent of how the network was initialized. In the paper we take the measured trajectory of one feature, multiply by the predicted ratios, and get the trajectories of the others with nothing fitted.

The cleanest way to say what is going on is by contrast with deep linear networks, where staged learning is a textbook phenomenon. There the input-output correlation is a matrix, with several singular directions, and different singular values escape at different times. Staging in that setting is a statement about rank. Here the output is scalar, so E[yx]\mathbb{E}[yx]E[yx] is one vector no matter how many nonlinear features the target contains, and there is exactly one direction available to grow. Nonlinearity on its own does not buy you staged learning. Rank does.

Two honest caveats. This covers the first escape only, and only the linear overlaps during it. Once the network leaves the small-signal region, the nonlinear part of the target can start driving directions that were invisible to E[yx]\mathbb{E}[yx]E[yx] the entire time, and whether those undergo their own critical-depth delays is exactly the question we don't answer.7 And the whole story needs Γ>0\Gamma > 0Γ>0: the target has to present some first-order correlation. If the linear part of the target vanishes identically, the leading-order drive is empty, and escape (if it happens) happens at higher order on a different clock entirely.

So, to summarize: small initialization does not impose some uniform tax on deep networks. The number of small layers fixes the exponent, the input-output correlation fixes the timescale, and the rank of the presented signal fixes how many directions are allowed to grow at once. Three quantities, two of which you can read off your dataset and your initialization scheme before training starts.8
